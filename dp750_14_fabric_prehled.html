Microsoft Fabric overview

Core concepts, SaaS architecture, OneLake, workloads, and differences from Azure Databricks
🗂 Bonus — outside the MOC➕ Custom module outside the official MOC

Chapter objectives

  • Describe what Microsoft Fabric is and how its SaaS model differs from the PaaS model of Azure Databricks
  • Explain the capacity model (F-SKUs, Capacity Units) including smoothing and throttling
  • Explain the role of OneLake and shortcuts — and how to reach data in Databricks from Fabric (and the other way round)
  • Map Fabric concepts onto the Databricks concepts you already know from chapters 1–13
  • Decide when Fabric makes sense, when Databricks does, and when a combination of both
14.1

What Microsoft Fabric is

Microsoft Fabric is an analytics platform delivered as SaaS — a single service covering the whole data lifecycle: ingestion, transformation, warehousing, streaming, data science, and reporting. You do not buy and manage individual Azure resources (a storage account, a Spark cluster, a SQL pool) — you buy one capacity and get everything inside it. No resource groups, no ARM, no VNets. You do not even need an Azure subscription to work — only to pay for the capacity.

For you as Databricks engineers the fastest mental model is this: Fabric is what you get when you take the Power BI service and attach a lakehouse, a warehouse, pipelines, Spark, and streaming to it — all on top of one shared storage. Fabric really did grow out of Power BI: the portal, workspaces, sharing, tenant administration — all of it is an evolution of the Power BI service, not a new product built on a greenfield.

ℹ️
Note: anyone expecting Fabric to be "Microsoft's version of Databricks" will be confused. Fabric is primarily a consolidation — Power BI + Synapse + Data Factory + Azure Data Explorer wrapped into one SaaS package. Databricks is an engine-first platform; Fabric is an integration-first platform. Most of the differences in this chapter follow from that.

The architecture has three layers: at the bottom OneLake (one logical data lake for the whole tenant), in the middle the platform services (governance through Purview, Copilot, security), and at the top the workloads — the individual "experiences" for different roles. Everything runs from one prepaid capacity:

The key words we will work with throughout the chapter: tenant (one organization, one OneLake), capacity (prepaid compute power), workspace (a container for work, assigned to a capacity), and item (anything you create in a workspace — a lakehouse, a notebook, a report…).

14.2

Capacity and Capacity Units — how you pay in Fabric

In Databricks you pay DBUs for specific running compute — a cluster, a SQL warehouse, a serverless job. When nothing runs, you pay nothing (apart from storage). Fabric does it the other way round: you buy a capacity in F-SKU sizes (F2 to F8192) and it represents a shared pool of Capacity Units (CUs) from which all operations of all workloads draw — a notebook, a pipeline, a SQL query, a report refresh. The capacity runs (and is billed) continuously until you pause it.

F-SKUCUIndicative price (PAYG)Note

Treat the prices as orders of magnitude — the state as of August 2026; verify the exact amounts in the Azure price list. An annual reservation gives roughly a 40 % discount. An important licensing threshold: from F64 up, users with a Free license can consume Power BI content; below F64 every report reader needs a Power BI Pro license.

Smoothing and throttling

How can an F2 with 2 CUs handle a Spark job that needs far more at peak? Through two mechanisms. Bursting lets an operation briefly exceed the capacity's base performance. Smoothing then spreads the consumption across a longer window for accounting purposes (24 hours for background operations) — the peak is "repaid" from quieter hours. Only when the accumulated debt outgrows the capacity does throttling step in — and gradually, not all at once:

A single greedy job can therefore push a capacity into Interactive rejection within a few minutes. The documentation simply calls that column Usage.
⚠️
Caution: a mental trap for Databricks engineers — throttling in Fabric is capacity-wide. One greedy Spark notebook can slow down the CFO's reports, because everyone draws from the same pool of CUs. In Databricks a cost explosion is isolated to a cluster/warehouse; here the only isolation is separate capacities.
💡
Tip: a capacity can be paused (UI or API) and is not billed while paused. A dev capacity running only during business hours costs a fraction. The main and quickest-to-reach tool for monitoring consumption is the free Fabric Capacity Metrics app (you can go deeper through workspace monitoring or Azure Monitor) — count on data latency in the order of minutes to tens of minutes.
How consumption is counted: CU-seconds and why Spark is "expensive"▼

Every operation burns CU × seconds of runtime. An F2 has a daily budget of 2 × 86,400 = 172,800 CU-seconds. From measurements in DP-600 classes: the same ingest of ~50 MB of CSV costs roughly 50–150 CU-s through a Pipeline Copy, 200–500 CU-s through Dataflow Gen2, but 800–1,500 CU-s through a PySpark notebook — the Spark session cold start dominates. For small data a "pure Python" notebook (Polars/DuckDB, no Spark cluster) is often cheaper in Fabric — a concept with no direct equivalent in Databricks. The orders of magnitude change with runtime versions, so take this as an illustration.

14.3

OneLake — one logical lake and shortcuts

OneLake is the storage automatically present in every Fabric tenant — built on ADLS Gen2, but fully managed: no storage accounts, no containers to create. From the documentation: "Every Microsoft Fabric tenant automatically includes OneLake… You can't delete OneLake or create multiple OneLakes." The analogy that works in class: OneLake is "OneDrive for data" — one namespace for the whole organization, with workspaces as folders inside it.

What matters for you: tables in OneLake are Delta Parquet — the same format you use in Databricks. All the Fabric engines (Spark, the T-SQL warehouse, KQL, Analysis Services for Power BI) read and write the same copy of the data; through Direct Lake mode Power BI can read the Delta files directly, without importing them into its own storage. OneLake also supports interoperability with Apache Iceberg through metadata virtualization and is accessible through the standard ADLS Gen2 API — so you can reach it from Databricks as well.

Shortcuts — zero-copy access to other people's data

A shortcut is a reference to data sitting elsewhere — in another workspace, in ADLS Gen2, Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, Dataverse, or S3-compatible storage. The data is not copied; in the lakehouse tree it appears as a local table or folder. Alongside shortcuts there is Mirroring — continuous replication of databases (Azure SQL, Cosmos DB, Snowflake, and also Azure Databricks Unity Catalog) into OneLake in Delta format.

The integration works the other way round too — from Databricks, OneLake behaves like an ordinary ADLS Gen2 endpoint:

PYTHON
# Databricks notebook: reading a Delta table stored in Fabric OneLake
# format: abfss://<workspace>@onelake.dfs.fabric.microsoft.com/<item>.<itemtype>/
# the workspace behaves as a container, the lakehouse as a folder
df = spark.read.format("delta").load(
    "abfss://Sales@onelake.dfs.fabric.microsoft.com/Analytics.lakehouse/Tables/orders"
)
display(df.limit(10))
💡
Tip: a shortcut is the cheapest "ingest" in Fabric — it consumes no CUs for copying, you pay only for the queries over the data. If you already have medallion layers in ADLS Gen2 managed by Databricks, a shortcut to the gold layer is typically the first and fastest step of integrating with Fabric.
⚠️
Caution: a shortcut only appears to bypass the source's governance — access is evaluated through the credentials stored with the shortcut. Whoever has access to the lakehouse sees the shortcut's data regardless of what Unity Catalog says on the Databricks side. The exact behavior of the security model (OneLake security, permission propagation) is still evolving — verify the state as of August 2026 in the documentation.
14.4

Workloads and items — what is in the box

Fabric organizes its features into workloads ("experiences") — sets of tools for a particular role. Every workload produces items in a workspace. An overview of the essentials (the state as of August 2026 — the list keeps expanding, so verify in the documentation):

WorkloadKey itemsDatabricks parallel
Data EngineeringLakehouse, Notebook, Spark Job Definition, Environmentnotebooks + clusters + Delta
Data FactoryPipeline, Dataflow Gen2, Copy jobLakeflow Jobs / ADF
Data WarehouseWarehouse (full T-SQL including DML)Databricks SQL warehouse
Real-Time IntelligenceEventstream, Eventhouse (KQL DB), ActivatorStructured Streaming + alerting
Data ScienceML Model, Experiment (MLflow inside)MLflow, Mosaic AI
Power BISemantic model, Report, DashboardAI/BI Dashboards (much weaker)
DatabasesSQL database, Mirrored databaseLakebase / source mirroring
Fabric IQOntology, data agent, Fabric Graph (preview)Genie / the agentic layer

Two things surprise a Databricks engineer. First, a lakehouse has two endpoints: Spark sees both Tables and Files, and the automatically created SQL analytics endpoint offers T-SQL reading of the same Delta tables — but read-only. Anyone wanting full T-SQL with INSERT/UPDATE needs a Warehouse item. Second, a default semantic model for Power BI is created automatically over every lakehouse and warehouse — good for a prototype, but production calls for an explicit model.

The engines differ, the data does not — so T-SQL can join items within one workspace with ordinary three-part naming:

SQL
-- Fabric warehouse: a join across a lakehouse and a warehouse in one workspace
SELECT o.order_id, o.amount_eur * fx.rate AS amount_usd
FROM lakehouse_sales.dbo.orders AS o
JOIN warehouse_finance.dbo.fx_rates AS fx
  ON o.currency = fx.currency;
⚠️
Caution: cross-database queries work only within the current workspace — the documentation says so explicitly: "You can write cross database queries to warehouses and databases in the current active workspace." Three-part naming does not cross workspace boundaries; make the target table available with a shortcut into a lakehouse in your own workspace first.
ℹ️
Note: Fabric adds workloads at the pace of several a year (most recently Databases and Fabric IQ, the latter in preview as of August 2026). Do not rely on a memorized list for the certification or in architecture — verify the current state in the documentation.
14.5

Mapping the concepts: Fabric ↔ Azure Databricks

The quickest way to find your feet in Fabric is to translate its concepts into the ones you know from chapters 1–13. Careful, though — some words (workspace, lakehouse) exist in both worlds but mean something different:

ℹ️
Note: the biggest false friend is lakehouse. In Databricks it is the philosophy of the whole platform; in Fabric it is one specific item type — essentially a "managed folder" with Tables and Files. The second is workspace: in Fabric it is a lightweight folder (dozens per tenant are fine), in Databricks a heavyweight resource with its own administration.
Common misconceptions on first contact with Fabric▼

"The SQL analytics endpoint is a warehouse." It is not — it is read-only. SQL developers regularly wonder why UPDATE does not work; writing into lakehouse tables belongs to Spark (Delta immutability + writes through the engine), and full T-SQL DML is only in the Warehouse item.

"Purview = Unity Catalog." Only roughly. Unity Catalog is one consistent permission model enforced by the engine (GRANT on catalog/schema/table, lineage, audit). In Fabric governance is assembled from several layers: workspace roles, item permissions, OneLake security, and Purview on top for labels, cataloging, and compliance. Stronger on compliance and M365 integration, more fragmented at the data-level ACL.

"Direct Lake = Import mode without refreshes, for free." Direct Lake reads Delta files straight into the memory of Analysis Services (so-called framing, without a copy), but with unsuitable data (high cardinality, many small files, DAX over an unsupported construct) it falls back to DirectQuery. The V-Order Parquet optimization helps considerably.

14.6

The fundamental differences — where the worlds part ways

SaaS vs. PaaS. Fabric takes the infrastructure decisions out of your hands: no choice of instance types, no per-cluster runtime versions, no VNet injection, no spot instances. That is a relief for teams without platform engineers — and a frustration for those used to tuning compute. In Databricks you pick the node type, autoscaling, Photon; in Fabric you get "some" Spark from a starter pool and the main lever is the size of the capacity.

Billing model. Databricks = you pay when something runs; costs grow with the workload and can be attributed to a specific job. Fabric = you pay for the capacity continuously; costs are predictable (a fixed monthly amount) but shared — and when you do not fit into the capacity, you deal with throttling or a scale-up of the whole capacity. The FinOps discipline moves from "switch clusters off" to "watch Capacity Metrics and isolate noisy workloads into their own capacities".

Governance. Unity Catalog is a unified, engine-enforced model. Fabric has a layered model (workspace roles → item permissions → OneLake security) plus built-in Purview for sensitivity labels, cataloging, and auditing — a strong integration with Microsoft 365 and compliance processes, but more places where permissions are defined.

Openness. Both platforms rest on Delta/Parquet and both speak Iceberg today (Fabric through metadata virtualization, Databricks through UniForm) — so the data is not locked in either world. The difference is in the engines and the ecosystem: Databricks builds on an open-source core (Spark, Delta, MLflow) and runs on three clouds; the Fabric engines (Warehouse, KQL, Analysis Services) are proprietary and the platform exists only as Microsoft SaaS.

Microsoft FabricAzure Databricks
Service modelSaaS — everything managedPaaS — you influence the infrastructure

Caution: "which is better" is the wrong question — the platforms aim at different centers of gravity. Fabric optimizes time-to-report and breadth of coverage for the whole organization; Databricks optimizes performance, scale, and engineering control. That is why the combination of both is so common in practice (see the next section).
14.7

When Fabric, when Databricks, when both

The decision is rarely "we will switch everything over". The typical guidance by the character of the team and the workload:

The combined pattern is worth spelling out, because it is the one you will meet most often: Databricks owns the processing (ingestion, quality, the medallion up to gold, ML features), the gold layer sits as Delta in ADLS Gen2 or directly in Unity Catalog, and Fabric owns the consumption — a shortcut or mirroring makes gold available in OneLake and Power BI runs over it in Direct Lake mode with no copying and no refresh windows. Each team stays in the tool it knows.

💡
Tip: in a combined scenario, watch two things: 1) V-Order — Parquet written from Databricks is not V-Ordered. Note, though, that V-Order is disabled by default even for newly created Fabric workspaces (spark.sql.parquet.vorder.default = false, for write performance), so the difference against Fabric is not automatic. Enable it deliberately where Direct Lake sits over the table and it is read considerably more often than written; 2) dual governance — agree that the source of truth for access to the gold layer is one side (typically Unity Catalog), and handle Fabric access at the workspace/item level.
And what about the cost of the combination? Am I paying twice?▼

Partly yes — you pay DBUs for the processing plus a Fabric capacity for the serving. In practice, though, a BI-only capacity comes out relatively small: shortcuts cost no CUs, Direct Lake eliminates refresh costs, and interactive queries are smoothed. The deciding factor is the F64 licensing threshold for free report readers — below it you pay Power BI Pro per user, which outweighs the price of the capacity with hundreds of consumers. Calculate both variants; verify the prices and rules as of August 2026 in the current price list.

14.8

Summary and resources

✓
Summary: Fabric is a SaaS analytics platform grown out of Power BI: one capacity (F-SKU, a shared pool of CUs with smoothing and gradual throttling), one OneLake (Delta Parquet, shortcuts and mirroring for zero-copy access — including into Databricks), and a set of workloads from Data Factory to Power BI. It stands differently from Databricks: SaaS instead of PaaS, a fixed capacity instead of pay-per-use, layered governance with Purview instead of Unity Catalog, integration instead of engineering control. In practice the combination usually wins: Databricks processes into gold, Fabric serves into Power BI through Direct Lake — and thanks to the shared Delta format both sides meet over a single copy of the data.
ℹ️
Note: Fabric changes faster than anything else we covered in this course — workloads, preview features, SKUs, and licensing rules. Everything in this chapter reflects the state as of August 2026; before making architectural decisions, go through the current documentation and release notes.
